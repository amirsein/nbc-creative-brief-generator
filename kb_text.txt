Introduction ▶
Welcome to Module 3 - Creating Winning Ads.
In this module we are going to take a deep dive into the modern way of running Meta ads.
Whether you’re brand new to Meta Ads or you’ve run campaigns before, this module will show
you the approach you should be taking to consistently get results in the current Meta ecosystem.
We’ll move away from outdated ideas around heavy targeting and focus on what really matters
now.
Creative, messaging, and how the algorithm responds to your ads.
By the end of this module, you’ll understand how to think about ad creation, testing, and iteration
in a way that aligns with how Meta delivers ads today and not outdated methods you’ve likely
seen or learnt before.
Modern Meta ⚡
In this video we’re going to talk about how Meta Ads actually work today and why the biggest
driver of performance is no longer targeting, but creative.
A lot of advertisers still approach Meta the way it worked years ago, focusing heavily on
interests, audiences, and complex targeting setups.
The reality is that Meta has changed.
Due to privacy updates, data restrictions, and the scale Meta now operates at, the platform
relies far more on its algorithm to decide who sees your ads.
Instead of you telling Meta exactly who to target, Meta now learns who responds to your ads
by analysing how people interact with your creative.
This means your ads themselves play the biggest role in determining performance.
Creative Targeting 🧱
To understand the way Meta operates today we first need to understand where it came from
and how it has developed.
In the past Meta relied heavily on audience targeting to decide who should see your ads.

Media buyers would build campaigns with multiple ad sets, each one targeting a different
interest, demographic, or lookalike audience.
The goal was to find small pockets of people who were likely to convert by narrowing
targeting as much as possible.
Most of the testing happened at the ad set level and not the creative level.
The same ads were often duplicated across multiple ad sets, with the only variable being the
audience.
If performance was poor, the assumption was that the targeting was wrong, not the ad.
This approach worked when Meta had access to far more user-level data and targeting
signals.
This structure also came with many downsides.
• Slow learning due to fragmented audiences
• Budget spread thin across many ad sets
• Limited creative testing
• Heavy manual optimisation
As privacy changes reduced the effectiveness of interest and demographic targeting, this
structure became less reliable.
Meta could no longer depend on narrow audience definitions to drive performance.
Because of this Meta had to come up with a new way to drive ad performance so that it
could still offer a reliable service to advertisers.

Instead of targeting people directly, Meta started learning who to show ads to based on how
people responded to the creative itself.
Creative is now the primary driver of Meta ad performance.
What Do We Mean By Creative 🖊📹🖼
Creative is a term you will hear a lot when it comes to ads and it’s really important we clarify
what we actually mean by this.
It’s not just the visual elements of an ad and how it looks and there are a number of
components that make an ad creative.
Creative is the full message being communicated to the person seeing the ad.
Meta doesn’t just analyse whether someone watched a video or clicked an image.
It analyses how people respond to the idea being presented.
Your creative is going to be made up of several elements working together.
• The hook
The first line, opening frame, or visual that stops the scroll.
• The angle
The perspective you’re taking on the problem, desire, or opportunity.
Different angles attract different types of people.
• The message
What you’re actually saying. The story, explanation, or promise being made.
• The framing
How the message is positioned. This could be educational, direct, emotional, logical,
aspirational, or problem-focused.
• The format
Video, static image, UGC, talking head, screen recording, text-led, carousel.
Different formats create different engagement patterns. In this module we will cover how to
create winning ads across both video and static formats, including how to write copy and
structure messaging for each.
• The call to action
What you’re asking someone to do next and how clearly that’s communicated.
These are all elements that influence how people interact with your ad.

We can have two ads that look really similar, but if the hook, angle or framing changes they
can perform completely differently.
This is why Meta can deliver these ads to completely different people.
We’re not just showing people different visuals to see what gets people taking action.
The algorithm is reacting to behaviour triggered by the different messaging.
Understanding this is critical.
Because when you change the creative, you’re not just changing how the ad looks. You’re
changing who the algorithm learns to show it to.
How Meta Uses Creative Signals to Find Buyers Today 🎣
Meta’s ad delivery system is built to constantly learn and adjust based on how people
respond to your creative.
At a high level, Meta looks at ad performance and uses those signals to decide who should
see your ads next.
Every interaction sends information back to the system.
This includes:
• How quickly someone stops scrolling
• How long they watch a video
• Whether they engage with the ad
• Whether they click
• Whether they convert
Meta is constantly looking at these signals and looking for patterns or user behaviour.
If people respond well it can then increase delivery of that ad to similar users. Or, if people
don’t respond well it can reduce delivery.
This is constantly happening in the background and is the system Meta now uses to drive all
ad performance.
You will likely have heard this referred to as ‘andromeda’.
Meta does not optimise around interests or demographics in the same way it used to.
It optimises around how people respond to ads.

When performance drops, it’s usually because the creative signals have weakened, not

Creative fatigue isn’t just people getting bored. It’s the point where the signals your ad sends

Understanding this changes how you approach testing, scaling, and optimisation.

AI Driven targeting ⚙
Meta has been gradually bringing in new AI tools into its system for a while now.
This includes things like:
- Adv+ campaigns
- Ad level AI tools
- AI bid adjustments
These tools have all been designed with Meta new ways of learning in mind.
Each one gives the algorithm more freedom to find different pools of people and learn about
where your ads will perform best.
When you heavily restrict targeting, you limit the number of people Meta can test your ads
against.
This slows learning and makes it harder for the algorithm to find patterns.
Broad audiences allow Meta to observe how different types of people respond to your
creative and quickly adjust delivery based on real behaviour.
Instead of pre-filtering people with interests or demographics, you let the algorithm do the
filtering for you.
Your ad is the filter.
If your ad and creative is strong you will naturally attract the right audience and repel the
wrong one.
When targeting is too narrow:
• Meta has less data to work with
• Learning takes longer
• Performance becomes unstable

Introduction ▶
In this video we are going to talk about Ad Angles.
An angle is all about framing the same offer in different ways, speaking to different emotions and
triggering different behaviours.
If our creative is how we target different audiences, then our ad angle is how we tell Meta who to
look for.
Different types of angles attract different types of people and this is crucial with Meta’s focus on
creative targeting.
I'm going to teach you what an ad angle actually is, how to think about angles strategically, and
how to use them to structure your creative testing.
My goal is to get you creating ads with intent and purpose and move away from random testing.
By the end of this video you should be able to break down any offer into multiple angles and
understand how to use them to drive performance on Meta.
What Is An Ad Angle? 📐
An angle is the specific way you present your offer, message or idea inside an ad.
We’re not talking about the product or the format here, but specifically the perspective you
choose to present it from.
Any product or service can be presented in multiple ways depending on what you choose to
emphasise.
It’s each of these presentations that is an angle.
These angles are incredibly important as everybody is going to have different needs, desires
and messaging that they resonate with.
Let’s use a fitness coaching business as an example and imagine they are selling a 30 day
body transformation package.
There are multiple ways we can present this.
- Speed > Get back in shape in 30 days without spending hours in the gym.
- Simplicity > A simple fitness plan for busy people who hate complicated workouts.
- Authority> The same system used by over 1,000 successful clients.

- Enjoyment> Get in shape using this fun and addictive workout routine.
- Benefit > Improve your body confidence with this workout routine.
These could all use the same format or same offer.
The thing that changes is the idea that is being communicated.
This can completely change the type of person that is attracted.
The outcome is the same but the mental entry point changes.
Angles vs creatives 📐 🖼
An angle is the idea
A creative is how that idea is expressed
One angle = Many creatives
One Creative = Only one angle
This also means that the same angle can be expressed as a video ad, a static image, or a
carousel.
The format changes. The core idea does not.
This is important to understand because later in the module we will look at how to write and
structure ads for both formats.
Creating Ad Angles For Your offer 🖊
When creating ad angles, we start by understanding the core problem our offer solves and
the different ways people emotionally experience that problem.
Rather than jumping straight into writing ads, we want to break our understanding down into
a set of clear inputs we can use to build strong angles.
These inputs act as ingredients. On their own they don’t create angles, but when combined
they form clear ideas we can test.
This gives us a structured way to approach ad creation instead of relying on guesswork or
random testing.
We can start by mapping these inputs and then work out how our specific offer fits into each
one.

Prioritising angles 📊
Not all angles are created equal and you will likely have more than you can realistically test
in one go.
You don't need to test everything at once and you’ll need to work out what to test first.
We can prioritise the order to test them using 3 simple filters.
1. Strength of the pain
Start with angles that connect directly to a strong, emotionally charged pain point.
If someone feels this problem regularly or it affects their confidence, energy, or daily life, it’s
usually a good place to start.
Strong pain = faster feedback.
2. The amount of people it applies to
Ask yourself how many people this angle could apply to.
Broad angles tend to work best early on because they give Meta more room to find
responders.
Highly specific angles can work well later, but they are usually better tested after you’ve
found something that performs at scale.

3. How believable it is
Prioritise angles that feel realistic and easy to accept.
If an angle requires a big leap of faith, heavy explanation, or sounds too good to be true, it’s
harder for people to engage with quickly.
Clear, believable ideas usually outperform clever or extreme ones.
When choosing your first angles to test:
- Pick 5 angles that score highly across all three filters
- Start with broad, obvious ideas before niche or complex ones
- Save abstract or advanced angles for later rounds of testing
We want to learn which angles meta responds to fastest and then we can build into more
complex ideas from there.
From our examples:
“Fear of staying overweight because they don’t have accountability and finally having a
system that keeps them consistent.”
This fits all three well.
- Being overweight is a strong pain
- Accountability applies to nearly everyone
- It’s believable as we have the mechanism to prove it.
We might want to avoid talking about “beach bodies” as this may only apply to a smaller
proportion of people who are going away or live near a beach.
Remember, this isn't our hook or ad copy yet. We will work on the language and wording
next.
Summary ✔
An angle is the specific perspective you choose to present your offer from. It is not the
format, not the hook, and not the copy. It is the core idea.
One angle can generate many creatives across many formats. One creative only ever
expresses one angle.

Introduction ►
In this video I’m going to teach you all about hooks.
A hook is the first thing someone sees or hears in your video ads, and it decides whether they
stop scrolling or ignore you completely.
This is usually going to be the first 1-2 lines of speech in your video ads but could also be the
main title in a graphic, or first line or two of text in an ad depending on what format you are using.
It doesn’t matter how good your offer is or how strong your angle is. If the hook doesn’t earn
attention no one will see the rest of the ad.
In this lesson I’m going to show you what a hook actually is, what its job is, and why most hooks
fail.
We’ll break down the difference between good hooks and bad hooks, how to avoid curiosity
clickbait that attracts the wrong people, and the common mistakes that kill ad performance.
I’ll also show you how to turn the ad angles you’ve already mapped out into clear, effective
hooks, using simple formulas you can apply over and over again.
The goal is to create hooks that immediately tell the right person, “this is for you”, and give Meta
the clear signals it needs to find more people like them.
By the end of this video, you should be able to look at any angle and confidently turn it into
multiple hooks that catch attention and set your ads up for success.
What Is A Hook 🎣
Put simply: your hook is the opening of your ad and its job is to earn a few seconds of attention
from the right person.
It’s not your headline and it’s not about trying to be clever, shocking or entertaining.
I want you to look at hooks as filters. A good hook doesn’t try to attract everyone. It tries to
quickly attract the right person and repel everyone else.
It quickly tells the viewer:
- What this ad is about
- Who it’s for
- Why they should keep watching
The hook is the most important part of your creative.

If the hook does its job properly the right people stick around for the full ad.
If it doesn’t they could potentially be scrolling past and the rest of the ad won’t have any chance
to perform.
Think of it like the cover of a book. It needs to catch the attention of the reader and give them an

The Job Of A Hook
Hooks have two main jobs to achieve.
- Clarity: The viewer to immediately understand what the ad is about
- Curiosity: The viewer feels like this is relevant to them and worth their attention.
If you have curiosity without clarity, people are confused.
If you have clarity without curiosity, people are bored.
By using a strong hook we balance both. It makes the viewer think:
“This is about my problem.”
“And this might actually help me.”

Why Hooks Matter On Meta 💭
Hooks are absolutely essential to ad performance on Meta.
This is because Meta looks at how people respond to your ad in the first few seconds.
- Who stops.
- Who watches.
- Who engages.
- Who leaves immediately.
Those early reactions tell Meta who your ad is relevant to and teaches it who to show your
ads to next.
This is why it's so important to make sure our Hooks are attracting the right people, and
filtering out people we don't want.
For instance, if we had a hook that was ‘clickbaity’ and drove a lot of curiosity but had no
clarity we could have a lot of irrelevant clicks.
This in turn provides bad feedback to the algorithm.
In short:
- Your angle decides who the ad is for.
- Your hook decides whether they pay attention.
If your hook is weak the angle never gets a chance.
Common Hook Mistakes ❌
Most hooks fail for one of four main reasons.
If you can identify which one is happening then fixing it becomes really obvious.
You don’t need to be clever, just keep it simple and avoid these four mistakes.
1. Delay ⌛
Delay happens when the hook takes too long to introduce what the ad is actually about.
The first line is vague, emotional, or “warming up” instead of giving context.
Examples of delay:
“This is one of the craziest things I’ve ever seen…”

“You won’t believe what happened when…”
These lines create curiosity, but they give the viewer no information to decide if they should
keep watching.
Lots of videos you see online suffer from this issue and now you are aware of it you will likely
see it everywhere.
On Meta, those first 1–2 seconds matter most. Every second without clarity causes people to
scroll past.
Why this kills performance:
If the viewer doesn’t know what the video is about immediately, they can’t decide to opt in.
Curiosity without context = uncertainty.
Uncertainty = scrolling.
How to fix it:
Make sure you introduce the topic in the first sentence
- Cut fluff.
- Cut warm-ups
- Increase your speed to value.
Good examples:
“If you’re struggling to lose weight, this is why it keeps coming back.”
“Here are three simple ways to lose fat without living in the gym.”

What this looks like
The hook introduces the topic, but the wording is unclear, overcomplicated, or awkward.
The viewer hears the words, but doesn’t fully understand what you mean.
This usually happens when:
- Sentences are too long
- Too many ideas are packed into one line
- Passive or abstract language is used

3. Irrelevance 🤷
Irrelevance happens when the hook is clear, but the viewer doesn’t feel confident the video
is for them.
They understand the topic, but they’re unsure if what’s coming next will actually help their
situation.
This often happens when hooks are framed around:
“I” and “me”
This happens when we focus on the creator’s story instead of the viewer’s problem or, use
generic statements instead of specific pain points.

Why this kills performance:
If the viewer doesn’t see themselves in the hook, they won’t invest attention.
They ask themselves:
“Is this actually relevant to me?”
If the answer isn’t immediately “yes”, they scroll.
How to fix it:
Frame hooks around the viewer.
- Use “you” and “your”
- Call out the situation or problem directly
- Make the expected value obvious

Instead of

Use
“If you struggle with weight loss…”
4. Disinterest 🥱
This happens when the hook is clear and relevant, but it doesn’t create enough curiosity to
keep someone watching.
The viewer understands what the video is about and who it’s for, but there’s no reason to
stay.
This usually happens when the hook:
- States a fact without tension
- Feels obvious or generic
- Lacks contrast
Why this kills performance:
Clarity and relevance get attention.
Curiosity keeps it.

Direct Response Principles (cid:0)
Direct response copywriting is a discipline built around one objective: getting the reader to

Remove All Ambiguity 💭
If a sentence can be interpreted in more than one way, rewrite it.
Ambiguity forces the reader to think, and the moment they have to think, they are deciding
whether to continue. Most of the time, they do not.
Every claim, every instruction, and every outcome in your copy should have exactly one
meaning.
Read each sentence and ask: could this be misread? Could someone understand this
differently to how I intended it?
If the answer is yes, simplify it.

It speaks directly and confidently to one person as though the entire ad was written
specifically for them.
When you write with these qualifications, it sounds like you are trying to avoid making a
promise you cannot keep.
The result is your copy sounds uncertain and unconvincing.
Instead, write to the ideal reader as if the result you are describing is their guaranteed reality.
Your angle has already done the work of attracting the right person so our ad copy needs to
speak to them directly and with conviction.

Describe the outcome in terms the reader already uses to think about their problem.

describe what they want.
If your clients say "I want more booked calls" then write "more booked calls," not "increased
pipeline velocity."
Clarity Over Cleverness 🤯
We need to make sure we are writing in a way that's easy for the reader to understand.
Wordplay, metaphors, and indirect language all slow comprehension.
In an environment where you have under two seconds to earn attention, anything that
requires the reader to decode what you are actually trying to say is an issue.
The best direct response copy is almost boring because it's so direct.
It says exactly what it means in the simplest language available.
If you find yourself proud of a line because it is clever, read it again and ask whether it is
clear.
Usually you will find you can make it shorter and more direct without losing anything.
Use Active Voice 💬
Active voice puts the subject first and the action second.
It is direct, fast, and confident.
Passive voice reverses this and produces copy that feels slow, weak, and uncertain.
Passive: "Results have been achieved by our clients across multiple industries."
Active: "Our clients get results across every industry we work in."
A good way to check if there is an issue is to read your copy out loud.
If a sentence sounds like it was written for a report, rewrite it in the most direct,
conversational way possible.

Copy Review Checklist 📋
Before publishing any ad copy, run it against this checklist.
1. Does the hook immediately signal who this is for?
2. Is every claim specific and quantified where possible?
3. Have I removed every sentence that can be interpreted in more than one way?
4. Am I speaking directly to one person or hedging for a crowd?
5. Does the copy tell the reader exactly what they will gain and exactly what to do next?
6. Is there at least one credibility signal; proof, authority, or a specific result?
7. Have I removed every word that does not add value or impact?
8. Does it read as fast and clearly out loud as it does on the page?
If you can answer yes to all of these, the copy is ready to test.

None of these work in isolation and the goal is to layer as many of these as possible into
your ad copy so that they have a compound effect.
These direct response principles remove every reason for the reader to hesitate.
- Write without ambiguity.
- Speak directly to one person. Use numbers.

- Choose clarity over cleverness.
- Write in active voice.

Writing Primary Text For Statics ✏
The primary text is where you build the case. The same three frameworks we used for video
scripts (Problem to Solution, Before After Bridge, and Myth to Truth) apply here. The
difference is in how you write them.
Static copy is written to be read, not heard. This means;
- Shorter sentences.
- Simpler words.
- No conversational filler.
In a video script you can afford transitions and breathing room because the viewer is
watching.
In a static the viewer is reading at their own pace, often while scrolling, and you lose them
the moment the copy becomes dense or effortful.
The most important rule for primary text: lead with your strongest idea.
The first line must do the most work. It is visible before the See More cut, which means it is
often the only line that gets read.
If that first line does not give the viewer a clear reason to keep reading, the rest of the copy
is invisible.

A strong opening line for static primary text identifies the specific person the ad is for, names
their problem or desire directly, and does it in as few words as possible. The goal is to make
the right person immediately think: this is about me.
From there, the body of the primary text follows whichever framework matches the angle.
Problem to Solution works well for pain-led angles where the viewer already knows they
have the problem.
Before After Bridge works well for transformation angles where you want them to feel the
contrast emotionally.
Myth to Truth works well for objection or identity angles where you need to shift a belief
before they will consider the offer.
Keep the overall length focused. Static primary text does not need to be long. Two to four
short paragraphs is usually enough.
End with a clear, direct call to action that tells the viewer exactly what to do next and why.
Writing Headlines That Drive Clicks ✏
The headline is the last thing the viewer sees before they decide whether to click. Most
advertisers treat it as an afterthought, usually defaulting to their brand name or a vague
description of the offer. This is a significant missed opportunity.
A strong headline does one of three things. It delivers a clear, specific benefit that makes the
offer immediately appealing.
It asks a direct question the viewer cannot help but answer yes to. Or it creates a specific
contrast that makes the offer feel like the obvious next step.
Headlines need to be short. Meta displays them in a limited space and long headlines get
truncated. Aim for six to ten words as a general rule. Every word should earn its place. If you
can remove a word without losing the meaning, remove it.
The headline and the primary text should not repeat each other. They are doing different
jobs. The primary text builds the case. The headline closes it. Think of the headline as the
last nudge before the click, not a summary of everything you just said.
Just like with video hooks and angles, you should test multiple headline variations against
the same creative. The image and primary text can stay the same while you test two or three
different headlines. Small changes here can have a meaningful impact on CTR without
requiring new creative production.

Start with the angle. Every decision flows from this. Know who you are targeting, what
problem or desire you are speaking to, and what the core idea of the ad is before you write a

Brief the image. Describe what you need visually in terms of who it should feature, what it

Write the text overlay if needed. Three to seven words. Clarity over cleverness. It should

Write the primary text. Lead with your strongest line. Follow the framework that suits the

elements work together as one unit.

action.

own right. Keep them short, specific, and focused on benefit, question, or contrast.
